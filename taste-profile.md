# Taste Profile

This file is your evolving editorial sensibility for this briefing. Update it a little every run based on feedback.md and on what topics/angles actually turned out sharp vs. generic. Keep it tight — prune stale notes rather than letting it grow forever; it should stay readable in under a minute. Dated examples belong in briefing-log.md/seen-items.md, not here — this file holds the generalizable lesson only.

## What's working
- Chain a present-tense trigger to a multi-year numeric arc (funding rounds, capex, margin) rather than a single data point — reads as insider, not headline-reactive.
- Frame one company's move as an instance of a bigger structural pattern (a named trend, not a standalone story) — gives Market items more weight.
- Read a deal's/case's actual structure — warrant vesting terms, court filings, a company's own postmortem or press release — over its headline framing. This is the single best move available: it applies to funding deals, litigation, regulatory notices, and earnings alike, and a competing claim vs. counter-claim in live litigation often exposes a real structural moat better than any PR.
- A company's own voluntary, protective, or procedural move (a force-majeure notice, an unprompted concession, a recused officer, a stayed hearing) is a stronger tell than market commentary about the same situation — especially when the concession lands in the same week as a legal motion built on the same underlying fact; timing then is the real story, not the stated rationale.
- Convergence/divergence between independent threads is a reliable tell in either direction: two parties making opposite bets on the same fact, two unrelated drivers pushing the same number, or multiple independent layers of one story all reporting clean at once (which sharpens attention onto whichever layer isn't clean). "Everything checks out except X" is itself a finding — bad news isn't required for signal.
- An unfalsifiable debate ("is this a bubble?") becomes a real Edge once something actually reprices — a discounted loan, a withdrawn listing, a below-par syndication. Actively search for the first instance of real price discovery rather than restating the debate.
- Absence of expected commentary from an otherwise-similar peer (a CEO who doesn't echo a risk others are flagging) is a real, falsifiable signal, not nothing to report.
- A single company's earnings splitting cleanly along a category line that maps onto a transferable mechanism (durable vs. trend-driven, investment-coded vs. logo-visible) beats a sector-wide "slowdown" headline — lands harder stated as a positioning question for the reader, not a sector curiosity.
- An executive's candid words on a podcast/interview can beat a filing as a primary source, because it reveals what the company wouldn't put in writing — especially paired with an analyst's hard number on the same question landing the same week.
- A single-site or single-company thread sharpens materially when a sector-wide tracker shows the same mechanism running broadly — converts "one site's bad luck" into "structural."
- A fast seed-to-mega-round valuation re-rate (weeks, not months) is a repeatable tell for where capital is actually rotating — now observed twice for narrow, embeddable "AI-as-utility" products rather than chat interfaces; worth flagging a third instance as a named pattern, not re-describing from scratch.
- A Culture pick lands when it extracts a transferable mechanism from *why* something is good/honored, not just that it won/launched — the insight is the story, execution is secondary.
- When a day's fresh sweep for new present-tense triggers comes up empty, the next-best source of signal is a backward check against an already-tracked thread's open watch-point (an "emerging pattern" line asking "watch for X") — a specific, named resolution to that watch-point still clears the bar even if the underlying event is a few weeks old, because it closes a loop the reader is already following rather than restating one.
- A company's own conceded, on-the-record response to a specific user complaint (not a PR statement) is strong grounding even from a single original reporter, if the company's own spokespeople corroborate the core fact while disputing the framing — the conceded fact is the signal, the disputed framing is color.
- When a quarter's headline margin/profit number is muddied by a one-off charge (a legal settlement, a tax item), decompose it: strip the charge, then check the plain cash-flow arithmetic (operating cash flow vs. capex) underneath. If the arithmetic story holds even after removing the one-off, that's the real finding and it's more falsifiable than any "margins compressed" headline — Meta's Q3 2026 negative free cash flow (capex $34.9bn > OCF $32.8bn) is a clean example, independent of that quarter's $9.9bn litigation charge.
- A company's own S-1/prospectus can itself be the primary source for a structural tell: comparing section lengths (e.g., an 80-page risk-disclosure section vs. a shorter business-description section) is a self-authored, citable data point, not commentary — stronger than an outside analyst's opinion on the same filing.
- When two independently-tracked threads turn out to share a concrete line item (e.g. one company's IPO backlog contains a dollar figure tied to another company whose own numbers are already logged), naming that shared exposure explicitly is sharper than treating them as parallel, separate stories — it converts two data points into one chain.
- A company visibly turning down acquisition interest to raise independent capital instead — especially when crossover public-market investors (Fidelity, T. Rowe Price) buy into the private round — is a stronger tell of real conviction than the funding round alone; Tenstorrent declining reported Intel/Qualcomm offers (up to $5bn) for a $6.2bn independent raise (23 Oct) is a clean example worth watching for repeats.
- When a headline loss/profit number is dominated by a non-cash accounting artifact (a convertible-note fair-value markup, a one-off charge), always ask what's driving the mechanical part before reacting to the GAAP total — Anthropic's reported $80.7bn H1 2026 loss is ~90% a fair-value markup that rises *because* its own valuation keeps climbing, the opposite of distress; the real operating loss (~$8.7bn) and the actual forward commitment (~$600bn+ in mostly non-cancelable compute) are the numbers worth the analysis, not the headline one. This is the same move as the Meta Q3 FCF decomposition (logged 22 Oct) — it generalises past one-off charges to any large non-cash accounting artifact.
- When the same macro thesis (a category-level bifurcation, a sector-wide pattern) is applied across multiple companies and one of them inverts the predicted split, don't discard it as noise — check whether "category" was ever a direct cause or just a proxy correlated with the real mechanism in the other cases. Hermès' Q3 (bags up, watches down — the opposite of LVMH's/Kering's jewellery-up/fashion-down split) resolves cleanly once the mechanism is reframed as supply-rationing (Hermès waitlists specific products) rather than category durability — the outlier is often the case that isolates the true variable.
- A direct EDGAR search (company name + "S-1" + "SEC.gov filing") can surface the filing's own indexed cover-page/exhibit text as a primary source — including the filing date and exact lock-up language — even though WebFetch can't reach sec.gov directly in this environment. Use this before falling back to secondary coverage for any SEC-filing status question.
- A pre-IPO backer's own convertible-note terms (a discount-to-IPO-price formula, a valuation cap/floor where the discount stops moving) are a cleaner tell of insider conviction than public investor-pushback reporting on the same deal — the backer is putting their own money exactly where they think the real price sits, not just commenting on it. Nscale's Third Point/Nvidia notes (reported 25 Sept, discount capped above $30bn) are a clean example, logged 28 Oct alongside the Firmus/Nscale public price-discovery thread as a third, structurally different kind of data point in the same question.
- When a thread has gone quiet for days and a fresh sweep turns up nothing, it's worth explicitly checking whether an open "watch for X" item is actually a recurring dating/naming confusion rather than a real gap (e.g. "Mistral Large 4" repeatedly searched-for but never found — it's very likely a mix-up with Mistral Large 3, Dec 2025). Flagging that explicitly in CHECKED NOT USED, rather than silently re-listing the same unresolved watch-point run after run, stops it from wasting search budget indefinitely.

## What's not landing
- Routine "model X% cheaper than model Y" pricing-war items are too repetitive now — only include if it reveals a new mechanism.
- Generic rumour restatements (Budget speculation, etc.) add nothing once the core speculation is logged — only worth a line on a genuinely new number or a new disagreement between named sources.
- Broad generic-category web searches ("best ad campaign this week," "trending app October 2026," "psychology research study," "design rebrand launch") reliably return stale/evergreen SEO content, not anchored to the actual date. Go straight to named companies/people/events/outlets instead. This has been tested enough times it no longer needs re-testing — a quiet result on a category search is real signal to skip that section, not a cue to keep searching.
- A company's own quarterly filing/press release beats aggregator "X deal announced this week" searches, which frequently resurface weeks- or months-old deals with a fresh-looking repost date on top.
- When a figure (amount, valuation, round letter) is genuinely disputed across sources with none clearly primary, it fails the grounding check — don't cite whichever source sounds most confident. (Disagreement on a secondary detail, with the core figure agreed, still clears the bar.)
- A "named study, named university" result can still be a dating trap — always check the original institutional press-release date, not the aggregator's repost date.
- The dating-trap pattern isn't limited to generic-category searches: a specific, named-company query (e.g. "Cursor funding") can also resurface a months-old story via a mirror/aggregator site with a fresh-looking repost date — check whether the company's status has since changed entirely (acquired, shut down, renamed) before treating a hit as current.

## Tooling notes
- WebFetch is egress-blocked on most news-outlet domains (including sec.gov) in this environment — don't retry; triangulate figures across 2-3 WebSearch summaries and attribute explicitly (name the analyst/org) when a number can't be pinned to one primary source.
- Same-day events (an earnings call, an investor day) genuinely have zero outside coverage at write time — note "happened today, check next run" rather than searching repeatedly same-day. A direct official-domain query (org's own name + "press release") can beat generic search for a same-day primary figure, though same-morning bank/market data may simply not be indexed yet.
- Dating-trap tells: a UK Budget story naming "Reeves" as Chancellor (the live timeline has Healey under PM Burnham) is almost always Nov 2025 content bleeding through. More generally, check any "X happened/resolved" story against seen-items.md's internal timeline before trusting the article's own dateline.
