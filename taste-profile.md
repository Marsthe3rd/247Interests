# Taste Profile

This file is your evolving editorial sensibility for this briefing. Update it a little every run based on feedback.md and on what topics/angles actually turned out sharp vs. generic. Keep it tight — prune stale notes rather than letting it grow forever; it should stay readable in under a minute. Dated examples belong in briefing-log.md/seen-items.md, not here — this file holds the generalizable lesson only.

## What's working
- Chain a present-tense trigger to a multi-year numeric arc (funding rounds, capex, margin) rather than a single data point — reads as insider, not headline-reactive.
- Frame one company's move as an instance of a bigger structural pattern (a named trend, not a standalone story) — gives Market items more weight.
- Read a deal's/case's actual structure — warrant vesting terms, court filings, a company's own postmortem or press release — over its headline framing. This is the single best move available: it applies to funding deals, litigation, regulatory notices, and earnings alike, and a competing claim vs. counter-claim in live litigation often exposes a real structural moat better than any PR.
- A company's own voluntary, protective, or procedural move (a force-majeure notice, an unprompted concession, a recused officer, a stayed hearing) is a stronger tell than market commentary about the same situation — especially when the concession lands in the same week as a legal motion built on the same underlying fact; timing then is the real story, not the stated rationale.
- Convergence/divergence between independent threads is a reliable tell in either direction: two parties making opposite bets on the same fact, two unrelated drivers pushing the same number, or multiple independent layers of one story all reporting clean at once (which sharpens attention onto whichever layer isn't clean). "Everything checks out except X" is itself a finding — bad news isn't required for signal.
- An unfalsifiable debate ("is this a bubble?") becomes a real Edge once something actually reprices — a discounted loan, a withdrawn listing, a below-par syndication. Actively search for the first instance of real price discovery rather than restating the debate.
- Absence of expected commentary from an otherwise-similar peer (a CEO who doesn't echo a risk others are flagging) is a real, falsifiable signal, not nothing to report.
- A single company's earnings splitting cleanly along a category line that maps onto a transferable mechanism (durable vs. trend-driven, investment-coded vs. logo-visible) beats a sector-wide "slowdown" headline — lands harder stated as a positioning question for the reader, not a sector curiosity.
- An executive's candid words on a podcast/interview can beat a filing as a primary source, because it reveals what the company wouldn't put in writing — especially paired with an analyst's hard number on the same question landing the same week.
- A single-site or single-company thread sharpens materially when a sector-wide tracker shows the same mechanism running broadly — converts "one site's bad luck" into "structural."
- A fast seed-to-mega-round valuation re-rate (weeks, not months) is a repeatable tell for where capital is rotating — track the speed explicitly, since a months-scale re-rate is a different, slower pattern and shouldn't be force-fit into the same bucket.
- A Culture pick lands when it extracts a transferable mechanism from *why* something is good/honored, not just that it won/launched — the insight is the story, execution is secondary.
- When a day's fresh sweep for new present-tense triggers comes up empty, the next-best source of signal is a backward check against an already-tracked thread's open watch-point (an "emerging pattern" line asking "watch for X") — a specific, named resolution to that watch-point still clears the bar even if the underlying event is a few weeks old, because it closes a loop the reader is already following rather than restating one.
- A company's own conceded, on-the-record response to a specific user complaint (not a PR statement) is strong grounding even from a single original reporter, if the company's own spokespeople corroborate the core fact while disputing the framing — the conceded fact is the signal, the disputed framing is color.
- A company's own S-1/prospectus can itself be the primary source for a structural tell: comparing section lengths (e.g., an 80-page risk-disclosure section vs. a shorter business-description section) is a self-authored, citable data point, not commentary — stronger than an outside analyst's opinion on the same filing.
- When two independently-tracked threads turn out to share a concrete line item (e.g. one company's IPO backlog contains a dollar figure tied to another company whose own numbers are already logged), naming that shared exposure explicitly is sharper than treating them as parallel, separate stories — it converts two data points into one chain.
- A company visibly turning down acquisition interest to raise independent capital instead — especially when crossover public-market investors buy into the private round — is a stronger tell of real conviction than the funding round alone.
- When a headline loss/profit number is dominated by a non-cash accounting artifact (a convertible-note fair-value markup, a one-off charge), always ask what's driving the mechanical part before reacting to the GAAP total, whether it's a litigation charge muddying a margin (Meta, logged 22 Oct) or a rising valuation inflating a convertible-note markup (Anthropic's $80.7bn H1 2026 loss, ~90% non-cash) — the real operating number underneath is what's worth the analysis, not the headline one. Generalises to any large non-cash accounting artifact.
- When the same macro thesis (a category-level bifurcation, a sector-wide pattern) is applied across multiple companies and one of them inverts the predicted split, don't discard it as noise — check whether "category" was ever a direct cause or just a proxy correlated with the real mechanism in the other cases. Hermès' Q3 (bags up, watches down — the opposite of LVMH's/Kering's jewellery-up/fashion-down split) resolves cleanly once the mechanism is reframed as supply-rationing (Hermès waitlists specific products) rather than category durability — the outlier is often the case that isolates the true variable.
- A direct EDGAR search (company name + "S-1" + "SEC.gov filing") can surface the filing's own indexed cover-page/exhibit text as a primary source — including the filing date and exact lock-up language — even though WebFetch can't reach sec.gov directly in this environment. Use this before falling back to secondary coverage for any SEC-filing status question.
- When a hyperscaler/mega-cap earnings search keeps returning the wrong fiscal period (a prior quarter's guidance, or a different company's fiscal-calendar offset), add the specific headline number you're trying to confirm (a dollar figure, a percentage) to the query rather than just the company+quarter — this reliably surfaces the actual post-release coverage (SEC 8-K exhibit text, outlet recaps with the real figures) where a bare "[company] Q3 2026 earnings" query keeps surfacing previews and guidance. Worked cleanly for Alphabet/Microsoft's 28 Oct reports this run.
- A valuation/price figure that reverses a very recently logged direction (a cut discussed, then un-cut a week later) is worth flagging even on thin, single-chain sourcing (one outlet relayed through aggregators) — but say so explicitly in the text rather than upgrading it to settled fact. The reversal itself, set against unchanged underlying fundamentals, is the real signal.
- A pre-IPO backer's own convertible-note terms (a discount-to-IPO-price formula, a valuation cap/floor where the discount stops moving) are a cleaner tell of insider conviction than public investor-pushback reporting on the same deal — the backer is putting their own money exactly where they think the real price sits, not just commenting on it. Nscale's Third Point/Nvidia notes (reported 25 Sept, discount capped above $30bn) are a clean example, logged 28 Oct alongside the Firmus/Nscale public price-discovery thread as a third, structurally different kind of data point in the same question.
- When a thread has gone quiet for days and a fresh sweep turns up nothing, check whether an open "watch for X" item might be a dating/naming confusion rather than a real gap — but verify with a product-specific query (model name + "preview"/"API"/"launch") before concluding that, not just a weights/release-date query. Mistral Large 4 was wrongly "ruled out" as a Large-3 mix-up on 28 Oct because the search only checked for shipped open weights; a direct query the next run found it had already launched as an API preview on 6 Oct. A real but partially-shipped product (preview live, weights pending) looks identical to "doesn't exist" if you only search for the part that hasn't landed yet.
- When a company's own executive pre-disclosed the mechanism behind a metric months before the metric moves (a CFO announcing an accounting-estimate change on one earnings call, a model-pricing change flagged in a prior deck), that forward disclosure is the key to decomposing the later quarter's headline number — check the prior call's transcript/coverage before taking a surprising beat or miss at face value. Microsoft's Q1 FY27 capex "beat" and positive FCF (28 Oct) traces directly to a useful-life/lease-accounting change CFO Amy Hood flagged on the July call, months ahead of the number landing.
- A political appointee's institutional vote against the very preference they were installed to deliver (Warsh voting for a Fed hike after being installed by Trump explicitly for lower rates) is a strong, falsifiable tell that data/institutional process is constraining political intent — and whether the appointer shields the loyalist from blame afterward (redirecting criticism elsewhere) versus attacks them personally is itself a secondary, readable signal of how real that constraint is.
- When a thread's only data so far has been a reported asking-price or analyst forecast, a company's own earnings release that discloses hard contract-coverage data (e.g. "LTAs cover 50%+ of 2027-28 capacity, sold out through 2029") is a genuinely new kind of data point even on an already-tracked topic — it's worth a fresh lead item, not folded into "checked, unchanged," because it's the first primary-source number in a thread that was previously all secondary-sourced.
- Setting a quarter's headline number against the company's own entire prior full-year record (not just a YoY%) makes an inflection concrete and checkable — e.g. "this single quarter's profit already exceeds the company's best full year on record" lands harder than another percentage, and is easy to verify once annual figures are in hand.
- When a multi-week thread has been built entirely from one side of a market (suppliers' asking prices, analyst forecasts), a major buyer's own earnings-call admission is categorically stronger grounding than another supplier data point — it closes the demand-side gap and is harder to dismiss as a seller talking its own book. Apple's 2 Nov call (Ternus calling memory pricing "unprecedented," $33bn purchase-obligation jump, three rounds of price hikes already taken) did this for the memory-squeeze thread tracked since 8 Oct, and is a repeatable move: when a thread is one-sided, actively search for the buyer's side before writing another seller-side update.
- A CEO publicly accusing an upstream industry of "hyping" scarcity to protect margins, arriving right as a major buyer's own executive calls the same scarcity "unprecedented," is a clean, dated Edge with two named, falsifiable positions — stronger than the generic bubble-debate format because both sides are quotable people with something to lose if wrong (Acer's Chen talking down prices he profits from being high; Apple eating the cost either way).
- When a company's own earnings release reports a segment's growth rate but not its dollar figure, applying that rate to a confirmed prior-year base and explicitly labeling the result as your own calculation (not company-disclosed) is a legitimate way to get a concrete number without failing the grounding check — AMD's implied ~$8.5bn Q3 2026 Data Center revenue (97% YoY on a confirmed $4.3bn Q3 2025 base) is a clean example. Only do this when both inputs are independently confirmed.
- A long AI-infrastructure-spend thread (capex, chips, memory, FCF) benefits from deliberately searching out the demand/application-layer counterpart — real enterprises paying for and expanding AI software, not just hyperscalers buying chips on a promise. Palantir's commercial-overtakes-government crossover (Q3 2026, logged 4 Nov) is the first clean example; naming the contrast explicitly ("infrastructure spend vs. proof of paying demand") gives an otherwise-familiar earnings beat real weight instead of reading as another AI-earnings item.
- When a long-tracked thread has been built entirely from separate cost-side and demand-side data points argued in isolation, a single trading session that nets them out into one verdict (a market selloff, a re-rating) is itself the sharpest kind of lead item — write it up on the magnitude and the market's own reaction (both primary-sourceable from multiple independent outlets) rather than waiting for the causal "why" reporting to fully resolve, since secondary causal framing (a regulatory angle, an election link) often stays genuinely contested across sources while the headline numbers don't. The 5 Nov 2026 Nasdaq correction, landing days after AMD's/Palantir's demand-side proof points, is a clean example — and it immediately re-priced a second, already-tracked thread (Anthropic's IPO) in real time, which is worth following into the next day's entry rather than treating as a one-off.
- When one search result's framing (e.g. "AI stocks hit records" vs. "AI stocks crash") flatly contradicts another for what should be the same date, check whether the bullish result is actually describing the prior day's session mislabeled with the wrong date — this happened with a post-midterm-election rally (4 Nov) vs. the correction that followed it (5 Nov) being conflated in search snippets. Resolve by searching for the specific day of the week plus multiple named mainstream outlets (CNBC, Fox Business, Motley Fool) rather than trusting either framing alone.
- When a prior day's entry flags a causal mechanism as "single lower-quality outlet, unconfirmed" (a regulatory/political angle behind a market move), searching the named political event directly (e.g. "Democrats take control of Congress 2026") rather than re-running the same market-recap query is what surfaces the better corroboration — AP-network-sourced election reporting plus a named analyst research note (UBS, via CIO Dive) resolved the AI-selloff/midterm-regulation link within one day of it being flagged as too thin to use.
- A state-level executive taking office on an explicit policy pledge (a governor-elect's own campaign video/platform) is a sharper, more durable regulatory-risk data point than a federal legislative threat on the same topic — no presidential veto applies, and a governor controls permitting agencies directly. When a "Congress might regulate X" thread is still abstract, check for a state-executive race that converts the same risk into something with real near-term teeth (Texas's governor-elect's AI-data-center moratorium pledge vs. the still-bill-less federal Democratic Congress thread, both Nov 2026, is the clean contrast).
- A vague "they'll push for oversight" thread sharpens materially once named bills get attached to named sponsors with a stated priority order (electricity costs over model-testing over liability, per Schumer) — the ranking itself is a data point, since it tells you which mechanism bites first and which has cross-party cover already (a blocked GOP ratepayer bill that drew some Democratic defections shows the electricity-cost angle isn't a clean party-line fight).
- When a regulated company (OpenAI) proposes its own version of regulation right as its opponents (Democratic sponsors) are drafting theirs independently, naming it as a pre-emptive framing move — shaping the definition before someone else does — is sharper than reporting the proposal at face value. Always check whether the outlet reporting it (Axios, here) discloses its own commercial relationship with the subject; state that caveat explicitly rather than silently trusting "exclusive" framing.
- Credit markets and equity markets pricing the same sector in opposite directions at the same time (AI-infrastructure bonds worsening while AI-application equity re-rates up) is a genuine, dated Edge in its own right — stronger than the generic bubble debate because it's two different classes of investor with different information sets reaching different conclusions, and it's falsifiable against the next earnings print (does the divergence close or widen).
- A fast seed-to-mega-round re-rate doesn't have to be weeks-scale to be worth logging — a months-scale re-rate (fal: $4.5bn→$8bn→$16bn over 11 months) is a different, slower pattern and should be labeled as such explicitly rather than force-fit into the weeks-scale "third instance" already flagged as a named pattern to watch for. Don't claim a pattern match that isn't quite there — say what's actually true (the revenue/customer grounding was the real story here, not the speed).
- A funding round with real named customers and a (even disputed-range) revenue trajectory is categorically stronger grounding than one with a valuation number and a strategic-partnership headline alone — when comparing two similar-looking mega-rounds in the same week, lead with the one that has a disclosed P&L and demote the other to Quick Hits rather than writing two equally-weighted deep dives.
- A company's own forward guidance for an upcoming print (not yet reported, but filed/disclosed ahead of the call) is legitimate grounding for a lead item even before results land — e.g. CoreWeave's own guided interest-expense-vs-operating-income gap, filed ahead of its 10 Nov earnings — because it's the company's own stated number, not analyst speculation; frame explicitly as "guided, not yet reported" and flag the actual print as a next-run follow-up.
- When a thread sits in "checked, unchanged" for many consecutive days on a company that has already filed a public document (an S-1, a prospectus), actively go back and read coverage of the filing's own contents once, rather than re-running the same headline/price-range search — Nscale's S-1 (filed 8 Oct, re-checked daily since as "no price range yet") had a genuinely sharp structural number ($103.4bn contracted vs $2.6bn active) sitting unused in plain sight for over a month.
- A binary status claim ("still pending," "not yet listed/ruled/signed") carried forward in the log for days or weeks is just as capable of going stale as a numeric figure — Shein's Hong Kong listing was wrongly carried as "no listing date found" for six days after it had actually listed and priced down ~73% from its old target; verify a long-open binary status directly (not just re-search the same query) before re-logging it as unchanged.

## What's not landing
- Routine "model X% cheaper than model Y" pricing-war items are too repetitive now — only include if it reveals a new mechanism.
- Generic rumour restatements (Budget speculation, etc.) add nothing once the core speculation is logged — only worth a line on a genuinely new number or a new disagreement between named sources.
- Broad generic-category web searches ("best ad campaign this week," "trending app October 2026," "psychology research study," "design rebrand launch") reliably return stale/evergreen SEO content, not anchored to the actual date. Go straight to named companies/people/events/outlets instead. This has been tested enough times it no longer needs re-testing — a quiet result on a category search is real signal to skip that section, not a cue to keep searching.
- A company's own quarterly filing/press release beats aggregator "X deal announced this week" searches, which frequently resurface weeks- or months-old deals with a fresh-looking repost date on top.
- When a figure (amount, valuation, round letter) is genuinely disputed across sources with none clearly primary, it fails the grounding check — don't cite whichever source sounds most confident. (Disagreement on a secondary detail, with the core figure agreed, still clears the bar.)
- A fast-moving consensus/odds figure (a poll, a vote-probability) already logged recently can go stale or turn out to have been misread — don't just restate an earlier run's number from memory; re-pull the actual poll/source each time and flag a discrepancy explicitly as a correction rather than silently carrying it forward or silently fixing it. BoE Nov-hike economist consensus (logged 3 Nov as "~1-in-8 expecting a hike," found 5 Nov to actually be 86% per the underlying Reuters poll) is the clean example — the two readings weren't a real divergence story, just a bad figure.
- A "named study, named university" result can still be a dating trap — always check the original institutional press-release date, not the aggregator's repost date.
- The dating-trap pattern isn't limited to generic-category searches: a specific, named-company query (e.g. "Cursor funding") can also resurface a months-old story via a mirror/aggregator site with a fresh-looking repost date — check whether the company's status has since changed entirely (acquired, shut down, renamed) before treating a hit as current.
- Before repeatedly re-checking a private company's quarterly figure against public-company peers (the Chanel-vs-LVMH/Kering tiebreaker, checked 5+ times since mid-Oct), verify the company's actual disclosure cadence once — Chanel reports annually only, confirmed 30 Oct — rather than re-searching for a quarterly number that will never exist.

## Tooling notes
- At session start, check `git status`/`git log --oneline --all` before editing: a fresh container has occasionally checked out `main` in a detached-HEAD state or behind origin, meaning a prior day's commit could sit unreachable from the `main` branch ref even though it reached origin fine. Fast-forward local `main` to match `origin/main` (or merge the detached commits in) before writing today's entry, so nothing gets orphaned or double-written.
- A stale article can carry a current-sounding headline while its URL path embeds the real (old) date — e.g. a Benzinga URL containing "/25/10/" meant 2025-10, not 2026-10, and its "record $8.02bn Q3 profit" figure was ~7x smaller than the genuinely current (2026) figure from other sources. When a search result's number looks anomalously small/large next to already-logged current data, check the URL's date segments before citing it.
- WebFetch is egress-blocked on most news-outlet domains (including sec.gov and huggingface.co) in this environment — don't retry; triangulate figures across 2-3 WebSearch summaries and attribute explicitly (name the analyst/org) when a number can't be pinned to one primary source.
- Same-day events (an earnings call, an investor day) genuinely have zero outside coverage at write time — note "happened today, check next run" rather than searching repeatedly same-day. A direct official-domain query (org's own name + "press release") can beat generic search for a same-day primary figure, though same-morning bank/market data may simply not be indexed yet.
- Dating-trap tells: a UK Budget story naming "Reeves" as Chancellor (the live timeline has Healey under PM Burnham) is almost always Nov 2025 content bleeding through. More generally, check any "X happened/resolved" story against seen-items.md's internal timeline before trusting the article's own dateline.
